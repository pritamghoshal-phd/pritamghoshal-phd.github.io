The tymbal of a cicada: nature's sound-generating metastructure

A species-adaptable mechanical model that explains how cicadas produce their remarkably loud, tonal calls — and why those calls differ so much between species.

Role: Lead author · Co-authors: R. Singh, H. Tao, E. Ganju, N. Chawla, J. M. Gibert, A. K. Bajaj
Bistable oscillators Metastructures Bioacoustics High-speed imaging MATLAB

The puzzle

A cicada a few centimetres long can produce a call audible hundreds of metres away. The sound comes from the tymbal — a ribbed membrane on the abdomen that buckles rapidly, snapping inward with a click, then relaxing. Individually those clicks are broadband and unremarkable. Collectively, filtered through the resonant air cavity of the abdomen, they become the clean tonal song we recognise.

The mechanism had been studied for decades, but a real gap remained: no unified framework explained the diversity of calls across species. Every species has a differently ribbed tymbal, a differently sized cavity, and a distinct song. Existing models were tuned to one species at a time and did not generalise.

Figure 1 — Anatomy of the sound organ

Figure 1. The tymbal sits dorsolaterally on the first abdominal segment of the male, just behind the wing base. It is a thin ribbed chitinous membrane — ribs roughly 50–60 µm thick joined by membrane only 20–30 µm thick — actuated by large tymbal muscles through an apodeme that pulls it inward. Ribs buckle in sequence from posterior to anterior, each snap producing a click. Behind the tymbal, the abdominal air cavity acts as a resonator: for the species studied here (Neotibicen canicularis) its volume of about 887 mm³ gives a resonant frequency near 3.8 kHz, which is what turns a broadband snap into a tonal call.

Experiments

The model was built against measurements rather than fitted after the fact.

  • High-speed imaging captured rib buckling as it happened
  • Laser vibrometry measured the tymbal's dynamic response
  • X-ray microscopy resolved the internal geometry non-destructively
  • Acoustic recordings across species provided validation targets
  • Cavity dimensions were measured to parameterise the resonators

This was genuinely collaborative work, spanning mechanical engineering, materials science and biology.

λ1 ← k1  ·  ζ ← c  ·  λ2 ← k2  ·  R̃1 ← r1  ·  R̃2 ← r2  ·  M ← m/m1

Figure 2. Each unit of the truss chain (orange) is a mass m that moves along the chain by uj, restrained by two tilted springs k anchored at pinned supports set at angle α0 and separated vertically by 2w0. That inclined geometry is what makes the unit snap: it is a von Mises truss, and the tilted springs carry the membrane's stretching. A Kelvin–Voigt element — spring k1 in parallel with dashpot c, both grounded — supplies the viscoelasticity of the chitinous resilin. Neighbouring masses are joined along the chain by springs r1, which carry the bending.

Each truss mass is tied through a rigid link and a spring k2 to a mass m1 of the resonator chain (blue), whose own neighbours are joined by springs r2. The red dashed box marks one unit cell. Red arrows mark the boundary conditions used in simulation: transmission at the left end (straight arrows) and reflection at the right (curved arrows). Coupling between the chains, k2 → λ2, is deliberately weak so the resonator filters the output without overwriting the truss dynamics; coupling within each chain (r1, r2) is larger so that waves can propagate.

Figure 3 — Asymmetric double well

Figure 3. Potential Π(U) = U⁴/4 − U³ + (λ1+λ2+2)U²/2 for the bistable unit, plotted here with λ1+λ2 = 0.2, together with the corresponding restoring force. Filled circles are the two stable equilibria, the open circle the unstable barrier between them. The well is asymmetric: the unbuckled state at U = 0 sits lower than the buckled state at U = 1.724, and the barrier seen from the buckled side is only 0.022 deep. That shallow return barrier is why the ribs snap back once the muscle relaxes, and why each rib can buckle and unbuckle several times within a single call.

Figure 4 — Simulating Eqs. (1)–(2) running

Figure 4. Eqs. (1)–(2) with the tymbal muscle added as a static pull F(T) on the truss equation, integrated live on a 20-cell chain. The muscle only ever pulls: F ramps up, holds, and is released to zero — there is no push in the opposite direction. What makes that work is that the rib is monostable when unloaded. With λ1+λ2 = 0.32 the chain is bistable only for F between 0.10 and 0.54; at F = 0 the buckled state does not exist at all, so the instant the muscle lets go every rib has nowhere to sit but flat and the chitin snaps them back. The cycle runs: contract, drive rib 1 past the barrier, let the front travel the chain leaving every cell latched, then release the moment it reaches the far end. Release is about 2.8× faster than buckling, matching the asymmetry in the paper. The bar at top left tracks the pull with the phase named beside it; the dashed levels and the amber tick on every gauge move as the load tilts the well, and both vanish at release when bistability disappears. Take Fhold below 0.32 and the front stops latching; above 0.54 the flat state disappears and the whole chain snaps at once with no wave.

Result

The model reproduces both the snap-through dynamics of the tymbals and the subsequent acoustic filtering, and — the part that mattered most — it is the first species-adaptable framework capable of reproducing the distinct song frequencies and call patterns observed across multiple cicada species. Change the rib count, spacing and cavity dimensions to match a species, and the correct song falls out.

Two results stand out. First, the truss chain supports a soliton-like travelling wave: a buckling front that moves along the chain carrying its own energy. Because the left boundary transmits rather than reflects, the system dissipates and the front is not a true soliton, but it behaves like one. Second, the band gap is created by the coupling itself — with the resonator chain detached, waves of all frequencies propagate; attach it and a gap opens whose width grows with coupling stiffness. Simulated spectrograms reproduce the two measured frequency bands and the gap between them.

For me the interesting part is the transfer. The same bistable-chain machinery I had been developing for reservoir computing turned out to describe an insect that has been solving the problem for millions of years. It also runs in the other direction: the tymbal is a working example of how to arrange bistable elements to shape a frequency response, which is a design lesson for engineered sound-generating and energy-harvesting structures.
