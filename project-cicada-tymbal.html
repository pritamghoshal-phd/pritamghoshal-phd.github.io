The tymbal of a cicada: nature's sound-generating metastructure

A species-adaptable mechanical model that explains how cicadas produce their remarkably loud, tonal calls — and why those calls differ so much between species.

Role: Lead author · Co-authors: R. Singh, H. Tao, E. Ganju, N. Chawla, J. M. Gibert, A. K. Bajaj
Bistable oscillators Metastructures Bioacoustics High-speed imaging MATLAB

The puzzle

A cicada a few centimetres long can produce a call audible hundreds of metres away. The sound comes from the tymbal — a ribbed membrane on the abdomen that buckles rapidly, snapping inward with a click, then relaxing. Individually those clicks are broadband and unremarkable. Collectively, filtered through the resonant air cavity of the abdomen, they become the clean tonal song we recognise.

The mechanism had been studied for decades, but a real gap remained: no unified framework explained the diversity of calls across species. Every species has a differently ribbed tymbal, a differently sized cavity, and a distinct song. Existing models were tuned to one species at a time and did not generalise.

Figure 1 — From anatomy to model

Figure 1. The mapping that made the model work. Each rib of the tymbal becomes a bistable oscillator that snaps through; the abdominal air cavity becomes a chain of resonators that filters the resulting broadband click train into a tonal call. Species differences enter as rib count, rib spacing and cavity dimensions — nothing else needs to change.

The modeling idea

We stopped treating the tymbal as a membrane and started treating it as a metastructure — a periodically arranged set of ribs with inherent frequency filtering, exactly the framing used for engineered metamaterials.

That reframing makes the model natural: a chain of bistable oscillators, one per rib, coupled to a chain of resonators representing the abdominal cavity.

  • Each rib buckles via snap-through — the same bistable dynamics as a von Mises truss
  • Sequential rib collapse sets the pulse train structure
  • The resonator chain performs the acoustic filtering
  • Geometry and material parameters map directly onto species differences

Experiments

The model was built against measurements rather than fitted after the fact.

  • High-speed imaging captured rib buckling as it happened
  • Laser vibrometry measured the tymbal's dynamic response
  • X-ray microscopy resolved the internal geometry non-destructively
  • Acoustic recordings across species provided validation targets
  • Cavity dimensions were measured to parameterise the resonators

This was genuinely collaborative work, spanning mechanical engineering, materials science and biology.

Figure 2 — Buckling to song, simulated running

Figure 2. Top: ribs buckle in sequence as the tymbal muscle contracts, each snap firing an impulse. Middle: the raw click train — broadband and toneless on its own. Bottom: the same signal after the resonator chain, now a decaying tonal burst. Changing rib count or cavity tuning changes the song, which is precisely the mechanism that distinguishes one species from another.

Result

The model reproduces both the snap-through dynamics of the tymbals and the subsequent acoustic filtering, and — the part that mattered most — it is the first species-adaptable framework capable of reproducing the distinct song frequencies and call patterns observed across multiple cicada species. Change the rib count, spacing and cavity dimensions to match a species, and the correct song falls out.

For me the interesting part is the transfer. The same bistable-chain machinery I had been developing for reservoir computing turned out to describe an insect that has been solving the problem for millions of years. It also runs in the other direction: the tymbal is a working example of how to arrange bistable elements to shape a frequency response, which is a design lesson for engineered sound-generating and energy-harvesting structures.
