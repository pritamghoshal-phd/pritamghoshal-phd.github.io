A robot that finds the bucket and lands the ball

A fixed course marked with a line, buckets set off to either side at varying distances, and one job: notice each bucket in time and put a ball in it. Line-following optional.

Competition: Mechatronics Competition, Purdue University · Scope: Mechanical design, electronics, control
Arduino Ultrasonic + IR sensing Servo actuation 3D printing Embedded control

The task

The robot had to traverse a fixed course on its own. The course was marked with a painted line, but following that line was a choice rather than a requirement — what was fixed was the path and where the buckets sat. Buckets were placed to either side of the line at varying offsets, and for each one the robot had to notice it was there and land a ball in it.

That splits into three problems that trade against each other. Navigation: track the line closely and you always know where you are, but you give up the ability to shorten the path or to stand off from a bucket to improve the shot. Detection: a bucket is only worth detecting if it is still in range by the time the firing solution is computed, so detection range and traverse speed are coupled. Ballistics: the launcher has a finite maximum range, and the further the bucket sits from the line, the more a small error in launch speed turns into a large error on the floor.

The last of those is the one that actually decides the score, and it is worth doing the arithmetic before building anything.

Figure 1 — The course

Figure 1. The course: a fixed path marked with a painted line, and six buckets set to either side at offsets from 0.9 m to 2.7 m. The robot carries a forward detection cone. Because line-following is optional, the interesting decisions are where to deviate and how close to pass a bucket — a nearer shot is a far more forgiving shot.

Mechanical design

The chassis had to carry the drive train, electronics and launcher while staying light enough to turn sharply and stable enough that firing did not disturb its heading.

Most components were 3D printed, which made iteration cheap. The launcher geometry in particular went through several revisions before projectile release became repeatable — early versions were accurate on the bench and erratic once the robot was moving.

  • 3D-printed chassis and custom mounts
  • Servo-driven launching mechanism
  • Sensor placement for complementary coverage
  • Mass distribution tuned for turning stability

Control logic

The Arduino ran a straightforward priority loop: avoid first, aim second, fire last. Obstacle avoidance always overrode targeting, because a collision ends the run while a missed shot does not.

The genuinely hard part was hysteresis. Naive threshold logic makes the robot oscillate at the edge of detection — steering away, losing the reading, steering back, re-detecting. Adding deadbands and committing to an avoidance manoeuvre once started fixed behaviour that no amount of threshold tuning could.

  • Real-time obstacle detection and path correction
  • Priority arbitration between avoidance and targeting
  • Hysteresis and deadbands to prevent oscillation
  • Firing gated on aim tolerance and range
Figure 2 — The firing solution

Figure 2. With the launcher h above the bucket rim, a ball leaving at speed v0 and angle θ lands at a distance that follows from 0 = h + d tanθ − gd²/(2v0²cos²θ). Solving for θ gives two answers — a flat one and a lobbed one — and the maximum range is (v0/g)√(v0²+2gh): about 2.88 m at 5 m/s. Two things follow. The far buckets at 2.7 m sit right at the edge of what the launcher can reach, so there is no margin. And sensitivity grows with distance: the same 5% error in launch speed puts the ball 1.5% long at half a metre but 8.8% long at 2.8 m. The flat solution is always the one to take — shorter flight time, less to go wrong.

Figure 3 — A runrunning

Figure 3. A full run. The robot traverses the course, and whenever a bucket enters the detection cone it computes the flat firing solution for that range and fires. Launch speed carries a ±5% scatter, as it does on real hardware, and a ball counts as landed only if it arrives within 14 cm of the bucket — roughly the mouth. Buckets turn green when landed, red when missed, and grey when they are simply beyond the launcher’s reach. The trades from Figure 2 show up directly: drop the launch speed and the far buckets go grey; raise the traverse speed and buckets leave the cone before a solution is worth taking; widen the detection range and you start engaging targets from further out, where the same speed scatter costs far more.

What stuck with me

This was a lesson in the distance between a control law that works in principle and one that survives contact with real sensors. Everything behaved on paper. The failures were all in the transitions — the moment an obstacle left the sensor cone, the instant firing recoil nudged the heading, the case where two obstacles were detected as one gap that was not actually there.

It is the same discipline my research needs, just with faster feedback: build the model, then find out where reality disagrees, then fix the model rather than the reality. A robot tells you within seconds. A viscoelastic truss can take considerably longer.
