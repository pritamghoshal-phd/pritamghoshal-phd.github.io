Autonomous object-targeting robot

Navigate a track, avoid whatever is in the way, find the target, hit it. Every part of that is easy in isolation and awkward once they have to run at the same time.

The build

The robot had to traverse a track autonomously, detect and avoid obstacles in real time, identify targets, and launch projectiles at them. No remote control and no map supplied in advance — every decision had to come from what the sensors reported at that instant.

Sensing used ultrasonic and infrared together, because each fails differently. Ultrasonic gives usable range but returns a wide cone, so it reports something is roughly ahead rather than exactly where. Infrared is narrow and fast but strongly dependent on surface reflectivity — a dark matte obstacle can read as much further away than it is. Combining them covered most of the cases where either alone would have driven the robot into something.

The launcher was built from servo motors, custom mounts and 3D-printed components, which let the geometry be revised between test runs in an afternoon rather than a week.

Figure 1 — Sense, decide, act

Figure 1. Ultrasonic and infrared sensors feed an Arduino running the navigation and targeting logic, which drives the wheel motors and the launcher servo. The two sensing modalities are deliberately complementary: ultrasonic gives usable range but a wide, imprecise cone, while infrared is narrow and fast but easily confused by surface reflectivity. Neither alone is dependable enough.

Mechanical design

The chassis had to carry the drive train, electronics and launcher while staying light enough to turn sharply and stable enough that firing did not disturb its heading.

Most components were 3D printed, which made iteration cheap. The launcher geometry in particular went through several revisions before projectile release became repeatable — early versions were accurate on the bench and erratic once the robot was moving.

  • 3D-printed chassis and custom mounts
  • Servo-driven launching mechanism
  • Sensor placement for complementary coverage
  • Mass distribution tuned for turning stability

Control logic

The Arduino ran a straightforward priority loop: avoid first, aim second, fire last. Obstacle avoidance always overrode targeting, because a collision ends the run while a missed shot does not.

The genuinely hard part was hysteresis. Naive threshold logic makes the robot oscillate at the edge of detection — steering away, losing the reading, steering back, re-detecting. Adding deadbands and committing to an avoidance manoeuvre once started fixed behaviour that no amount of threshold tuning could.

  • Real-time obstacle detection and path correction
  • Priority arbitration between avoidance and targeting
  • Hysteresis and deadbands to prevent oscillation
  • Firing gated on aim tolerance and range
Figure 2 — Navigation and targeting, runningrunning

Figure 2. A top-down simulation of the control logic. The robot follows the track, sweeps its sensor cone for obstacles, steers around them, and fires when a target falls inside its aiming tolerance. The cone turns amber when something is detected. Widen the obstacle field and the avoidance behaviour starts competing with progress along the track — which is exactly the trade-off the competition scoring rewarded getting right.

What stuck with me

This was a lesson in the distance between a control law that works in principle and one that survives contact with real sensors. Everything behaved on paper. The failures were all in the transitions — the moment an obstacle left the sensor cone, the instant firing recoil nudged the heading, the case where two obstacles were detected as one gap that was not actually there.

It is the same discipline my research needs, just with faster feedback: build the model, then find out where reality disagrees, then fix the model rather than the reality. A robot tells you within seconds. A viscoelastic truss can take considerably longer.
