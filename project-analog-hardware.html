Reservoir computing, soldered onto a circuit board

Three op-amps and two multipliers per oscillator, wired into a chain and etched onto a board. The same physical substrate — used as a surrogate reservoir — performs chaotic prediction, closed-loop control, image classification and damage detection.

Why a surrogate

Physical reservoir computing usually asks the system you care about to be the computer. That often fails in practice for two reasons: not all of a real system’s states are accessible, or they cannot be sampled fast enough; and the system may simply lack the nonlinearity or the depth of memory the task needs.

This work takes the other route. A secondary system — a surrogate — does the computing, and it only ever needs to be characterised against its own inputs. That shifts the engineering problem away from tailoring a bespoke reservoir for every application and toward choosing the input encoding, the target formulation and the reservoir parameters. One architecture, many tasks.

The substrate is a chain of bistable viscoelastic oscillators — the same von Mises truss with a standard linear solid that runs through my truss work and my metamaterial inverse design. Bistability supplies the nonlinearity, viscoelasticity supplies the memory, and the balance between them is tuned by three numbers.

Figure 1. Each unit cell is a mass m on inclined springs k set at angle α0 and separated by 2w0, carrying a grounded viscoelastic element — spring k1 in parallel with a Maxwell arm (k2, c) — and coupled to its neighbours by r1. The input ũ(T) reaches every node through a binary mask, riding on a sinusoidal carrier. Only the displacements are read out, and the weights come from ridge regression, so training touches nothing but a linear layer. The second equation is the standard-linear-solid constitutive law: Σ relaxes to X, so it contributes memory during transients without moving the equilibria.

Figure 2. Equations (1)–(2) integrated live on a 24-node chain with Ω = 2.5 and F0 = 0.1. Amber nodes are the ones the binary mask connects to the input; the rest see only the carrier. Below, the node displacements — the only quantity the readout ever sees. Push β up and the chain retains more of its history; push R1 up and the nodes lock together and the usable state rank collapses.

Four tasks, one substrate

Each task is posed as a time-series input–output mapping, which is what lets a single architecture cover all of them. Chaotic prediction reproduces the Lorenz attractor. Closed-loop control settles onto a reference trajectory. Digit images are resized to 10×10, flattened into a sequence and classified at 91.65% on unseen data. And damage is detected by subdividing the chain into overlapping segments, each acting as its own small reservoir, then comparing prediction error between the healthy and damaged system across the chain.

The costs are competitive rather than revolutionary, and the paper says so. Against an MLP on time-series prediction the reservoir trains in 6.5 s versus 28.1 s and uses half the memory; against a CNN on images it is slightly slower. The argument is not that it wins on benchmarks — it is that one substrate covers all four.

Figure 3 — Tasks, parameters and cost

Figure 3. The same chain, re-parameterised per task. Note that the carrier frequency Ω = 2.5 and forcing F0 = 0.1 are held fixed throughout — what changes is the chain length, the memory (β), the stiffness ratio (λ) and the coupling. Spectrogram analysis during training shows why no single parameter set is optimal: performance degrades whenever a broad band of frequencies is excited, and improves when the response concentrates near the fundamental, a sub-harmonic or a super-harmonic. Prediction wants fundamental content; control wants fundamental plus harmonics; classification and damage detection want near-fundamental. The bistable nonlinearity, the viscoelastic memory and the coupling together decide how input energy is redistributed — and the right redistribution is task-specific.

Figure 4 — Finding the damagerunning

Figure 4. This runs the paper’s actual method in your browser, at reduced scale. The chain is split into overlapping segments — the segment centred on node i spans [i−M, i+M] — and each segment is trained as its own small reservoir on a temporal XOR benchmark, a task that cannot be solved without memory. Damage is one weakened coupling spring, R1 → ΔR1. Every segment readout is fitted twice, once on the healthy chain and once on the damaged one, and the bars show where prediction error rose. With β = 0.9 the rise concentrates on the segments containing the damage — typically 75–85% of it inside ±10 nodes. Drag β to 0 and the chain becomes purely elastic (Σ collapses to X, no memory), no segment can solve XOR in the first place, and the error difference scatters across the whole index range at under 10%. That is the paper’s Figure 7 result: memory is what makes damage locatable, not merely detectable. 80 nodes and M = 8 here against 400 and M = 20 in the paper; 640 segment readouts are fitted per run (160 per realisation, averaged over four input draws to suppress single-draw noise), so it takes a moment.

It exists as hardware

The governing mechanical equations were converted to their electrical analog and etched onto a printed circuit board. Each oscillator is three op-amps and two multipliers; the binary mask is a physical switch that either connects a node to the input or does not. A sinusoidal carrier and Lorenz-derived inputs were supplied, and outputs recorded, through a dSPACE 1104 controller, then split 80:20 for training and testing.

A five-oscillator chain reproduced the overall trend of the Lorenz trajectory, with visible deviation. Two honest reasons: the board was repurposed from an earlier study so its resistor values do not correspond to the simulated parameters, and noise accumulates along the chain. Scaling to many oscillators is the open hardware problem — the routes suggested are pairing a small analog chain with next-generation reservoir computing, or miniaturising into MEMS.

Figure 5 — The board

Figure 5. The signal path on hardware. The mask is a switch, not a multiplication in software; the nonlinearity is a multiplier, not a call to pow. That is the whole point — the dynamics happen in the circuit, and the computer only fits the linear readout afterwards.
