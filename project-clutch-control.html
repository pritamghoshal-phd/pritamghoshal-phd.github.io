Robust control of stick-slip instability in automotive clutches

Clutch squawk is a friction-induced instability, not a manufacturing defect. Treating it as a dynamics problem rather than a damping problem is what makes it controllable.

Role: Lead inventor · Status: U.S. Patent Application No. 63/989902 (pending)

The problem

Slipping clutches sometimes emit a loud, unpleasant groan during engagement — squawk. It is one of the more stubborn NVH problems in a driveline, because it is not caused by a part being out of tolerance. It is a self-excited vibration.

The mechanism is a falling friction characteristic. If the friction coefficient decreases as relative sliding velocity increases, then a small increase in velocity reduces the resisting force, which increases velocity further. Formally the system acquires negative damping: the steady-sliding equilibrium loses stability and the response grows until it settles onto a stick-slip limit cycle. That limit cycle is what you hear.

Conventional fixes attack the symptom — friction modifiers, additional damping, changed facing materials — and tend to trade the noise against durability or engagement feel. I approached it as a control problem instead: if the instability arises from a lack of damping in a specific mode, the right response is to supply damping there, actively and only when needed.

Figure 1 — Where the squawk comes from

Figure 1. During slow engagement the disc and flywheel are in frictional contact under normal force N, with the disc restrained by driveline stiffness k and damping c. The friction coefficient follows a Stribeck law, μ(v) = μk + (μs − μk)e−|v|/vs: it starts at the static value and falls toward the kinetic one as sliding speed rises. The shaded region is where dμ/dv < 0 — and a resisting force that decreases as velocity increases is, formally, negative damping.

Modeling

The engagement was reduced to a lumped torsional model: an inertia in frictional contact with a driven surface, restrained by driveline compliance, with a velocity-dependent friction law.

Reduced-order models like this are essential here — a full finite element model cannot be integrated over the many parameter combinations needed to map a stability boundary.

  • Stability analysis of the steady-sliding equilibrium
  • Continuation of the limit cycle amplitude across parameters
  • Identification of the conditions under which squawk appears
  • Sensitivity to normal load, engagement rate and stiffness

Control

The controller injects damping into the unstable mode, targeting the negative-damping term directly rather than adding broadband dissipation that would degrade engagement quality.

Robustness mattered more than optimality: the friction characteristic varies with temperature, wear and contamination, so the algorithm had to hold up under substantial uncertainty in the very parameter driving the instability. That robustness requirement is the core of the patent application.

  • Damping injection targeted at the unstable mode
  • Robust to variation in the friction characteristic
  • Validated in simulation across the engagement envelope
  • U.S. Patent Application No. 63/989902
Figure 2 — When does it squawk?

Figure 2. Linearising about steady sliding gives θ̈ + (2ζ + dμ/dv)θ̇ + θ = 0, so a falling friction curve contributes negative damping. Below a critical sliding speed the total goes negative, the steady-sliding equilibrium is lost, and the response grows until it settles onto a stick-slip limit cycle. With these parameters that threshold sits at v* ≈ 0.62 — slower than that and it squawks, faster and it does not. Injecting damping into the unstable mode lifts the whole curve, which is what the controller does: it does not flatten the friction characteristic or add broadband dissipation, it moves an eigenvalue.

Figure 3 — Stick-slip, with the controller on and offrunning

Figure 3. The lumped torsional model integrated live with a Stribeck friction law and a true stick phase — the interface locks whenever the required friction torque falls inside the static limit, and the block turns amber while stuck. Left: the engagement, with the effective damping reported live. Right: the phase portrait; uncontrolled it winds outward onto a large stick-slip limit cycle around the steady-sliding point, controlled it spirals in. Below: the radiated waveform. Raise the sliding speed past v* ≈ 0.62 and the squawk disappears on its own, which is why the problem is worst during slow engagement. Then set the speed low again and toggle the controller — the limit cycle collapses and the waveform goes quiet without touching the friction characteristic.

Why it matters

Squawk sits at the intersection of tribology, structural dynamics and control, which is why it resists single-discipline fixes. Framing it as a stability problem makes the solution space much clearer: you are not trying to make the clutch quieter, you are trying to move an eigenvalue.

The same framing generalises. Brake squeal, machine-tool chatter and hose-clamp groan are all friction-induced instabilities with the same underlying structure, and a controller that supplies damping robustly against an uncertain friction law is applicable to all of them.
