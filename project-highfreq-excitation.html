Shaking a bistable truss into the state it cannot rest in

A von Mises truss has two stable states and one unstable one in between. Shake its base sideways fast enough and the unstable state becomes stable — without changing the structure at all.

Role: Lead author · Co-authors: J. M. Gibert, A. K. Bajaj · Venue: JCND 20(8), 081004
Direct separation of motions Averaging Pitchfork bifurcation Multistability MATLAB

The idea

Fast vibration does strange things to slow dynamics. A pendulum hung upside down will stand up if you shake its pivot quickly enough — Kapitza's result from 1951. The fast motion does not average to nothing; it averages to an extra stiffness, and that stiffness can change which states the system is willing to sit in.

This paper asks what that does to a structure that already has more than one state to choose from. The von Mises truss is the simplest such structure: two inclined springs meeting at a mass, stable at X = 0 and X = 2, unstable at X = 1 — the configuration where the bars lie flat and the slightest push sends the mass to one side or the other. Instead of pushing the mass, we shake the base: one pin slides horizontally at y = Y₀ cos(ΩT).

Figure 1 — The system

Figure 1. The mass m is constrained to move only vertically in a slot, so the single degree of freedom X is its height. The dashpot c is the only dissipation. The right pin is grounded; the left pin slides horizontally under the base displacement y = Y₀ cos(ΩT). Lengths are scaled by α₀w₀, so X = 0 and X = 2 are the two stable configurations and X = 1 is the flat, unstable one. The amplitude is bounded by Y₀ < 1/α₀, which keeps the moving pin inside the truss width.

What the shaking actually does

The equation of motion in nondimensional form is

X″ + 2ζX′ + X³ − 3X² + [ (Y₀²/2)cos²(ΩT) + (Y₀/α₀)cos(ΩT) + 2 ] X − (Y₀²/2)cos²(ΩT) − (Y₀/α₀)cos(ΩT) = 0

which looks unpromising until you notice that every term carrying a cosine shares a factor. Collect them and the equation becomes

X″ + 2ζX′ + X(X−1)(X−2) + g(T)·(X−1) = 0,    g(T) = (Y₀²/2)cos²(ΩT) + (Y₀/α₀)cos(ΩT)

I checked this rearrangement against the printed equation at ten thousand random states; it agrees to 10⁻¹⁴. And it says something the original form hides: the base excitation enters only as a time-varying stiffness attached to the distance from the middle state. Whatever the shaking does, it does it by pulling on X − 1.

Separating the motion into a slow part z and a fast part φ riding on it, with φ = (Y₀/α₀)(z−1)cos(ΩT) + (Y₀²/16)(z−1)cos(2ΩT), and averaging over the fast scale replaces g(T) with a constant:

z″ + 2ζz′ + z(z−1)(z−2) + G·(z−1) = 0,    G = Y₀²/4 + Y₀⁴/(128Ω²) + Y₀²/(2α₀²Ω²)

The first term is just the mean of g; the other two are the correction from the fast motion feeding back on itself. Because the (z−1) factor survives averaging, the equilibria factor too:

(z − 1)(z² − 2z + G) = 0   ⟹   z = 1,   1 ± √(1 − G)

and the whole result falls out of that one line. The middle state is always an equilibrium. Its stiffness is G − 1: negative — unstable — when the shaking is weak, and positive once G passes 1. The outer pair have stiffness 2(1 − G), so they exist and are stable only while G < 1, and as G climbs they slide inward and merge into the middle state exactly when it turns stable. That is a supercritical pitchfork, and the threshold is simply G = 1.

A note on the algebra. The paper prints the O(1/Ω²) term two different ways in Eqs. (14) and (16). Only the Y₀⁴/(128Ω²) form reproduces the paper's own quoted threshold of Y₀cr = 1.758 for α₀ = 30°, Ω = 5 — it gives 1.7570, against 1.7588 for the other. The figures below use the Y₀⁴ form throughout.

Past that the truss has one stable state and it is the one it could never hold when still. Lowering α₀ or Ω reaches the threshold at much smaller amplitude — the Y₀²/(2α₀²Ω²) term dominates for a shallow truss.

Figure 3. Both curves start at X = 2, the paper's initial indentation. Blue is the full equation, teal dashed the averaged one. The fast ripple visible on the blue trace is the φ correction: measured peak-to-peak it sits within 3–8% of the predicted (Y₀/α₀)(z−1)/Ω² amplitude. Below threshold the mass settles into an outer well and the two curves agree to about 1%. Press Y₀ = 2.0 and both collapse onto X = 1.0000 — the flat configuration, held stable by nothing but the shaking.

Where averaging stops being reliable. I ran both equations from thirteen starting positions spanning X₀ ∈ [−0.4, 2.4]. Far below threshold they are excellent: at Y₀ = 0.3 all thirteen land in the same well and the equilibrium positions agree to 0.0007. Above threshold all thirteen agree again, because there is only one well left. But near the bifurcation — Y₀ between about 1.4 and 1.76 — only five to seven of the thirteen land in the same well, even though the predicted equilibrium positions are still accurate to about 0.01. The averaged equation tells you where the states are; close to the pitchfork it does not reliably tell you which one you will end up in, because the fast ripple is large enough to carry the mass across a basin boundary that the averaged picture has smoothed away.

Figure 4. A textbook supercritical pitchfork, read right to left in stability: the two outer equilibria 1 ± √(1−G) exist only while G < 1, curving in and annihilating against the middle branch at the threshold, which then continues alone as the single stable state. The grey region marks Y₀ > 1/α₀, where the sliding pin would leave the truss width and the model no longer describes the hardware. For α₀ = 30° the threshold at 1.757 sits just inside that limit of 1.91, which is why the paper's benchtop model needed the shallow 5° truss to show the effect — there the threshold drops to 0.59.

Why it matters

A multistable structure normally commits to a well and stays there. This says you can choose the well — and even choose a configuration that is not a well at all when the structure is at rest — using nothing but a shaker and the right amplitude. No latch, no actuator at the mass, no change to the structure.

The paper backs the analysis with a LEGO benchtop model. At α₀ = 5° the middle state was stabilised, with large amplitude at 30 Hz and small at 50 Hz; at α₀ = 30° it was not — consistent with the threshold, which that geometry puts out of comfortable reach.

This sits inside a longer thread on the same structure: the viscoelastic von Mises truss, where the material rather than the excitation supplies the extra timescale, and reservoir computing, where a chain of these elements is trained to compute.
