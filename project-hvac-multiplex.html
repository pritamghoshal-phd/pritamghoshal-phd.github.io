HVAC design for a three-screen multiplex

A cinema is a deceptively hard load case: dense but intermittent occupancy, tall volumes, concentrated equipment heat, and an audience that notices every draught.

Competition: ISHRAE National Student Design Competition · Scope: Load calculation, duct design, CFD verification

The brief

Design the heating, cooling and ventilation system for a three-screen multiplex: size the plant, lay out the distribution, and demonstrate that the result actually delivers comfort rather than merely meeting a load on paper.

Auditoria are an unusual space type. Occupant density is among the highest in any building, but it swings from near-empty to full within minutes. Each person contributes both sensible and latent heat, so a packed screening is a fundamentally different thermal problem from a sparse one. Ceilings are tall, which encourages stratification. Projection equipment adds a concentrated load in one place. And comfort expectations are strict — people sit still for two hours, so draughts and cold spots that would pass unnoticed elsewhere generate complaints here.

Cinema auditoria are an unusual load case: occupant density is very high but intermittent, ceilings are tall, and projection equipment adds concentrated heat, so the peak for each screen depends heavily on scheduling.

Load calculation

Heating, cooling and ventilation loads were computed for each auditorium separately rather than for the building as a whole, because the screens do not fill and empty together.

Fresh-air requirements scale with occupancy and, in a densely occupied space, ventilation becomes a large share of total cooling — conditioning outdoor air is often more demanding than offsetting the envelope gain.

  • Sensible and latent gains from occupants
  • Envelope conduction and solar gain
  • Projection equipment and lighting
  • Fresh-air requirement by occupancy
  • Per-screen peaks and diversified building peak

Distribution and verification

The duct network was modelled in SolidWorks, sized for acceptable velocity and pressure drop, and arranged so that branches could be balanced.

CFD was the part that made the design credible. Meeting a calculated load says nothing about where the air actually goes; simulation exposes short-circuiting between supply and return, stagnant zones at the back rows, and stratification in a tall raked volume — all failures that satisfy the spreadsheet perfectly.

  • Duct layout and sizing in SolidWorks
  • Velocity and pressure-drop checks per branch
  • CFD of air circulation within each auditorium
  • Verification of thermal comfort at seating level
  • Stratification and draught-risk assessment
Figure 2 — Load build-uprunning

Figure 2. The cooling load assembled contribution by contribution as occupancy rises. Drag the slider and watch the occupant terms — sensible and latent — separate and then dominate everything else. That is the whole difficulty of an auditorium: the envelope, the lighting and the projection load barely move, but the people are most of the load and they arrive all at once. A nearly empty screening and a full one are, thermally, two different buildings.

What I took from it

This was my first substantial exposure to the gap between a load calculation and a working system. Sizing the plant is arithmetic; making a room comfortable is a flow problem, and the two are only loosely connected. CFD was where that distinction became concrete for me.

It also made the case for zoning. Because occupants dominate the load, screens must be controlled independently — a single system sized for simultaneous full occupancy is oversized nearly all of the time, and oversized equipment cycles badly, dehumidifies poorly and wastes energy.

The habit of validating an analytical result against a simulation, and treating disagreement as information rather than nuisance, is the same one I rely on in my doctoral work.

Figure 3 — Air in the room, solved liverunning

Figure 3. What this actually solves. It is a two-dimensional incompressible flow solver on a 118×48 grid across a section of the auditorium. Each step: occupant heat is added to a temperature field at seat level; buoyancy is applied as a Boussinesq body force, so warm air accelerates upward in proportion to how much hotter it is than setpoint; velocity is advected by semi-Lagrangian back-tracing (Stam’s stable-fluids scheme, which is why it never blows up); a pressure field is solved by 16 Jacobi iterations and subtracted, which is what makes the flow divergence-free — air is neither created nor destroyed; then temperature is advected through that velocity field and lightly diffused. Supply diffusers inject cold air with downward momentum, returns apply suction. The colour is temperature relative to setpoint; the streaks are massless tracers riding the velocity field. What it is not: there is no turbulence model, no radiation, no moisture transport, and the units are non-dimensional — it is a section, not a validated CFD run. It gets the qualitative behaviour right, which is what the comparison below depends on. Toggle the strategy: overhead mixing throws cold air down through the whole volume and pushes warm air back into the seated zone, while underfloor displacement lets the occupant plumes carry heat up to a high-level extract. At a full house, head-height temperature sits about 0.8 lower under displacement for the same supply condition, and the stratification flips sign.

Figure 4 — What the coil has to dorunning

Figure 4. The same design drawn on a psychrometric chart, computed from the Magnus saturation relation rather than read off a printed one — 25 °C at 50% RH comes out at 9.86 g/kg and 50.3 kJ/kg, which matches the tables. Outside air mixes with return air in proportion to the fresh-air fraction, the mixed condition is dragged down to the coil (blue), and the supply air then picks up the room’s sensible and latent gains on the way back to the room condition (amber). The reason auditoria are awkward shows up here: people are a heavily latent load, so the coil SHR falls and the coil spends much of its duty wringing moisture out rather than dropping temperature. Push the fresh-air fraction up — which a full house demands for ventilation — and the coil duty climbs steeply, because you are now cooling outdoor air rather than recirculated room air.
