Exploiting bistability and viscoelasticity in reservoir computing

Mechanical systems have no delay line. Viscoelasticity is the only way a structure can remember — so I built a reservoir computer around it, first in simulation and then in analogue hardware, three op-amps and two multipliers per oscillator, etched onto a board.

Role: Lead author · Co-authors: J. M. Gibert, A. K. Bajaj · Hardware: IDETC 2026, supported by NSF CAREER CMMI-2145803

The problem

Reservoir computing lets a physical system do the heavy lifting of machine learning. Instead of training a deep network, you feed a signal into a nonlinear dynamical system, let its own physics transform the input into a rich high-dimensional response, and train only a simple linear readout. The appeal for mechanical engineering is obvious: computation with almost no power and no processor.

But a reservoir has to satisfy one hard requirement — it must retain recent inputs while gradually forgetting older ones. In electrical implementations this fading memory is trivial to arrange with a delay line. In a mechanical system there is no equivalent. Viscoelasticity is the only available mechanism, because a viscoelastic structure's state depends not just on how it is deformed now but on how it has been deformed.

Figure 2 — The unit cell, liverunning

Figure 2. The unit cell, running the real equations. Left: a von Mises truss. Right: a standard linear solid, spring k1 in parallel with a Maxwell arm (k2, η); its end node follows U while the internal junction follows Z, which lags — that visible gap across the dashpot is the fading memory. Below: the potential is not a symmetric double well. The quadratic nonlinearity of the truss contributes a −U³ term, giving Π(U) = U⁴/4 − U³ + (λ+2)U²/2 with equilibria at U = 0 and 3/2 ± √(1−4λ)/2 — bistable only for λ < ¼. U = 0 is the natural state (state A, apex up) and it is the deeper of the two; the snapped state at 3/2+√(1−4λ)/2 (state B) is metastable, which is why the truss snaps back. With λ = 0.15 the barrier sits at 1.184 and the snapped state at 1.816. The solid curve is this relaxed potential and its wells do not move. The faint dashed overlay is the instantaneous potential the mass feels at the current Z: because the viscoelastic force β(Z−U) is non-zero during transients it tilts the landscape, which is the memory made visible. It relaxes back onto the solid curve whenever Z catches up to U.

The reservoir

The unit cell is a bistable von Mises truss coupled to a standard linear solid element. Chained together, these form the reservoir.

  • Bistability supplies strong nonlinearity through snap-through between wells
  • The standard linear solid supplies fading memory with a tunable relaxation time
  • Governing equations derived from first principles
  • Nondimensionalised in terms of the Deborah number and dimensionless stiffness and viscoelastic parameters

Nondimensionalising early was the decision that made the rest tractable — it collapsed a large parameter space into the handful of ratios that actually control behaviour.

Evaluating it

Performance was measured against tasks, not aesthetics:

  • Information rate for discrete tasks such as logic operations
  • Normalised mean-square error for continuous prediction tasks
  • Memory capacity and autocorrelation as direct measures of short-term memory
  • Largest Lyapunov exponent to detect when the reservoir had gone chaotic

Parameter sweeps mapped how performance varies with viscoelastic and excitation parameters, including the finding that near resonance a monostable cell outperforms a bistable one.

The trade-off, made explicit

The central result is that memory and nonlinearity pull against each other, and the Deborah number sets where you sit between them. Increase the relaxation time and memory improves while the useful nonlinearity washes out; shorten it and the reverse happens. There is no universally optimal setting — the right balance depends on the task, and the paper shows how to tune toward it deliberately rather than by search.

Push too far and the unit cells become chaotic, at which point prediction collapses entirely. To characterise that boundary analytically rather than empirically, I developed a framework based on Melnikov's method that predicts the transition from optimal to poor reservoir performance. It gives a design criterion instead of a warning after the fact.

A physical breadboard implementation was built to check that the behaviour survives contact with real hardware, and it reproduced logic operations with reasonable fidelity.

The same chain, used as a surrogate

Physical reservoir computing usually asks the system you care about to be the computer. That often fails in practice for two reasons: not all of a real system’s states are accessible, or they cannot be sampled fast enough; and the system may simply lack the nonlinearity or the depth of memory the task needs.

This work takes the other route. A secondary system — a surrogate — does the computing, and it only ever needs to be characterised against its own inputs. That shifts the engineering problem away from tailoring a bespoke reservoir for every application and toward choosing the input encoding, the target formulation and the reservoir parameters. One architecture, many tasks.

The substrate is the chain above — the same bistable von Mises truss carrying a standard linear solid, the one that also runs through my truss work and my metamaterial inverse design. Nothing about the physics changes. What changes is the job: instead of asking the structure under study to be the computer, a separate chain does the computing and the structure merely drives it.

Figure 4. Each unit cell is a mass m on inclined springs k set at angle α0 and separated by 2w0, carrying a grounded viscoelastic element — spring k1 in parallel with a Maxwell arm (k2, c) — and coupled to its neighbours by r1. The input ũ(T) reaches every node through a binary mask, riding on a sinusoidal carrier. Only the displacements are read out, and the weights come from ridge regression, so training touches nothing but a linear layer. The second equation is the standard-linear-solid constitutive law: Σ relaxes to X, so it contributes memory during transients without moving the equilibria.

Four tasks, one substrate

Each task is posed as a time-series input–output mapping, which is what lets a single architecture cover all of them. Chaotic prediction reproduces the Lorenz attractor. Closed-loop control settles onto a reference trajectory. Digit images are resized to 10×10, flattened into a sequence and classified at 91.65% on unseen data. And damage is detected by subdividing the chain into overlapping segments, each acting as its own small reservoir, then comparing prediction error between the healthy and damaged system across the chain.

The costs are competitive rather than revolutionary, and the paper says so. Against an MLP on time-series prediction the reservoir trains in 6.5 s versus 28.1 s and uses half the memory; against a CNN on images it is slightly slower. The argument is not that it wins on benchmarks — it is that one substrate covers all four.

Figure 6 — Tasks, parameters and cost

Figure 6. The same chain, re-parameterised per task. Note that the carrier frequency Ω = 2.5 and forcing F0 = 0.1 are held fixed throughout — what changes is the chain length, the memory (β), the stiffness ratio (λ) and the coupling. Spectrogram analysis during training shows why no single parameter set is optimal: performance degrades whenever a broad band of frequencies is excited, and improves when the response concentrates near the fundamental, a sub-harmonic or a super-harmonic. Prediction wants fundamental content; control wants fundamental plus harmonics; classification and damage detection want near-fundamental. The bistable nonlinearity, the viscoelastic memory and the coupling together decide how input energy is redistributed — and the right redistribution is task-specific.

Figure 7 — Finding the damagerunning
Damage localisation results: the error difference between reservoir segments identifying which node is damaged.

Figure 7. This runs the paper’s actual method in your browser, at reduced scale. The chain is split into overlapping segments — the segment centred on node i spans [i−M, i+M] — and each segment is trained as its own small reservoir on a temporal XOR benchmark, a task that cannot be solved without memory. Damage is one weakened coupling spring, R1 → ΔR1. Every segment readout is fitted twice, once on the healthy chain and once on the damaged one, and the bars show where prediction error rose. With β = 0.9 the rise concentrates on the segments containing the damage — typically 75–85% of it inside ±10 nodes. Drag β to 0 and the chain becomes purely elastic (Σ collapses to X, no memory), no segment can solve XOR in the first place, and the error difference scatters across the whole index range at under 10%. That is the paper’s Figure 7 result: memory is what makes damage locatable, not merely detectable. 80 nodes and M = 8 here against 400 and M = 20 in the paper; 640 segment readouts are fitted per run (160 per realisation, averaged over four input draws to suppress single-draw noise), so it takes a moment.

It exists as hardware

The governing mechanical equations were converted to their electrical analog and etched onto a printed circuit board. Each oscillator is three op-amps and two multipliers; the binary mask is a physical switch that either connects a node to the input or does not. A sinusoidal carrier and Lorenz-derived inputs were supplied, and outputs recorded, through a dSPACE 1104 controller, then split 80:20 for training and testing.

A five-oscillator chain reproduced the overall trend of the Lorenz trajectory, with visible deviation. Two honest reasons: the board was repurposed from an earlier study so its resistor values do not correspond to the simulated parameters, and noise accumulates along the chain. Scaling to many oscillators is the open hardware problem — the routes suggested are pairing a small analog chain with next-generation reservoir computing, or miniaturising into MEMS.

Figure 8. The signal path on hardware. The mask is a switch, not a multiplication in software; the nonlinearity is a multiplier, not a call to pow. That is the whole point — the dynamics happen in the circuit, and the computer only fits the linear readout afterwards.
