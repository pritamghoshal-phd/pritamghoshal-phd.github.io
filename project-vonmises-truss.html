Dynamics of the viscoelastic bistable von Mises truss

What happens when a structure that can snap between two stable states is also made of a material that remembers. The foundational thread of my doctoral work.

Role: Lead author · Co-authors: J. M. Gibert, A. K. Bajaj, Q. Zhao
Harmonic balance Bifurcation analysis Chaos Viscoelasticity MATLAB

Why this system

The von Mises truss is about as simple as a bistable structure gets: two pinned bars meeting at an apex that can snap from one side to the other. Despite that apparent simplicity it represents a broad class of systems — shells, arches, buckling beams — and it permits lumped-parameter analysis where a continuum model would be intractable.

Add viscoelasticity and it stops being simple. The mechanical motion is governed by a second-order Duffing-type equation with quadratic nonlinearity, while the viscoelastic force evolves under a first-order equation on a much slower timescale. Combined, they form a third-order jerk system — and the interaction between the fast elastic oscillations and the slow material relaxation produces behaviour neither equation shows alone.

Periodic response & stability

Int. J. Non-Linear Mechanics, 166:104858 (2024)

Using the harmonic balance method to capture the interplay between geometric and material parameters under harmonic forcing. Principal findings:

  • Viscoelasticity introduces additional timescales and degrees of freedom
  • The system supports multiple coexisting periodic solutions
  • An equivalent viscous damping approximation fails to reproduce the dynamics
  • An optimal configuration exists for maximum energy dissipation — directly useful for vibration suppression

The presence of chaos

J. Computational & Nonlinear Dynamics, 19(7):071009 (2024)

Establishes that the harmonically forced viscoelastic truss is genuinely chaotic, not merely complicated. The slow–fast structure of the jerk system is what creates the route in.

This result mattered beyond its own paper: it defined the boundary I later needed for reservoir computing, where the goal is to operate near the edge of chaos without crossing it.

High-frequency base excitation

J. Computational & Nonlinear Dynamics (2025)

High-frequency excitation is known to stiffen systems, bias them and smooth discontinuities. In a multi-stable system those effects become far more consequential, because the bias toward one equilibrium depends strongly on the combination of excitation and geometric parameters.

Practically: you can steer which well the structure prefers without changing the structure.

Effect of boundary conditions

ICNDA, Springer Proceedings · with Q. Zhao

The earliest piece of the thread. Examines how boundary conditions reshape the bistability and stability landscape of the truss, establishing the modelling assumptions the later journal papers depend on.

Where it led

Taken together these papers characterise a system that is nonlinear, multi-stable, chaotic under the right forcing, and possessed of genuine short-term memory through its material. That combination is unusual — and it is very close to the specification for a physical reservoir computer.

That realisation became the next thread of my research: if the viscoelastic bistable truss already has nonlinearity and fading memory, can a chain of them be trained to compute? The answer, developed in the reservoir computing work, is yes — within parameter windows this earlier analysis is what let us find.
