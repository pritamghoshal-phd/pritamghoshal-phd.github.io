Vehicle–trailer dynamics and regenerative suspension

Suspension dampers exist to destroy energy. If you are going to destroy it anyway, it is worth asking how much can be recovered instead — and what towing does to the answer.

Role: Modeling and simulation · Tools: Abaqus, Simulink, MATLAB
Vehicle dynamics Abaqus Simulink Energy harvesting Structural vibration

Why the trailer changes everything

A conventional damper converts suspension motion into heat and discards it. A regenerative suspension replaces that damper with an electromagnetic transducer, so the same relative motion produces electrical power. The engineering tension is immediate: the transducer must still supply the damping the vehicle needs for ride and handling, and those two requirements do not automatically agree.

Adding a trailer makes it considerably harder. A hitch couples two sprung masses that would otherwise be analysed independently, creating modes belonging to neither vehicle alone — pitch coupling, yaw interaction, and the low-frequency motions responsible for trailer sway. Energy transfer runs in both directions across the hitch, so the trailer is not simply a passive follower.

I modelled the coupled system to answer two questions together: how much energy is genuinely available at each axle over realistic road inputs, and how much damping can be diverted to harvesting before ride and stability degrade.

Modeling approach

Two levels of fidelity, used for different questions. Abaqus captured structural flexibility and the vibration modes of the bodies themselves; Simulink handled the coupled multi-body dynamics and the energy-transfer path fast enough to sweep parameters.

The road input matters as much as the model. Real profiles are broadband and stochastic, so single-bump responses give a misleading picture of average recoverable power.

  • Abaqus models for structural vibration
  • Simulink for coupled vehicle–trailer dynamics
  • Quarter-car reduction for parameter sweeps
  • Randomly generated road profiles as excitation

What the model reveals

Recoverable power scales with suspension velocity, which means rough roads and higher speeds help — but those are also the conditions where ride quality is least negotiable.

The hitch turns out to be the interesting element. It transmits motion in both directions, so trailer behaviour feeds back into the towing vehicle, and there are speed and loading combinations where the coupled pair performs worse than the vehicle alone.

  • Energy availability across the operating envelope
  • Damping split between ride control and harvesting
  • Hitch force as a two-way coupling path
  • Sensitivity to trailer loading and tow speed

The trade-off

Harvesting more energy means extracting more force per unit of suspension velocity, which stiffens the damping the occupants feel. There is a genuine optimum rather than a free lunch, and where it sits depends on the road spectrum, the vehicle, and — with a trailer attached — on load distribution as well.

The useful conclusion for design is that the harvester should not be tuned as a fixed component. The conditions offering the most recoverable energy are the same conditions demanding the most from the suspension, so a semi-active strategy that shifts the split according to what the road is currently doing outperforms any single passive setting.
