Bio-inspired mechanical metamaterials for vibration and wave control

Structures whose vibration behaviour comes from how they are arranged rather than what they are made of — and biology has been arranging them well for a very long time.

Role: Design, characterisation and experimental validation · Lab: Advanced Dynamics and Mechanics Lab, Purdue

The idea

A mechanical metamaterial gets its properties from geometry and periodicity rather than from bulk material composition. Arrange identical unit cells in a repeating pattern and the assembly develops frequency ranges — band gaps — in which elastic waves simply cannot propagate. Drive the structure inside a gap and the disturbance decays exponentially with distance instead of travelling.

The version I work with is locally resonant: each cell carries a small internal oscillator tuned to the frequency you want to block. Because the gap is governed by that local resonance rather than by cell spacing, attenuation is possible at wavelengths much longer than the lattice itself. That sub-wavelength behaviour is what makes the approach practical for low-frequency vibration, where conventional isolators become impractically bulky.

The bio-inspired part matters. Natural structures — moth wings, spider webs, the ribbed tymbal of a cicada — routinely combine periodicity, bistability and graded properties to shape their acoustic response. Those architectures are worth borrowing, and they connect this thread directly to the cicada work.

Figure 1 — Unit cell and band structure

Figure 1. A periodic chain of masses with attached local resonators. Below the resonator's natural frequency the lattice transmits freely; near it, energy is trapped in the resonators instead of propagating, opening the shaded band gap where no travelling wave exists. Because the gap is set by the local resonance rather than the lattice spacing, it can sit far below the wavelength you would normally need — this is the sub-wavelength attenuation that makes metamaterials useful.

Design and characterisation

Cell geometry was tuned so that the band gap covers the frequency range of interest, then the assemblies were fabricated and measured rather than trusted to simulation.

Silicone casting was used for compliant cells; material characterisation mattered because polymer stiffness and loss are strongly frequency dependent, and a model using a single static modulus will predict the wrong gap.

  • Finite element dispersion analysis
  • Silicone casting and 3D printing of specimens
  • Material characterisation across frequency

Experimental validation

Transmission through fabricated lattices was measured directly, giving the frequency ranges where energy fails to pass through.

Laser Doppler vibrometry gives full-field motion without adding mass to a lightweight specimen — important, since a contact accelerometer can detune the very resonators being measured.

  • Laser Doppler vibrometry for full-field response
  • Experimental modal analysis
  • Measured transmission versus predicted band gaps
  • Model correction where measurements disagreed

Vibration control and energy harvesting

The two applications turn out to be the same physics read in opposite directions. For attenuation, you want energy trapped in the resonators and kept away from the protected structure. For harvesting, you want energy concentrated in resonators you have instrumented, so that a transducer sees far larger local motion than the ambient field would otherwise provide.

Both benefit from the same design lever: place the local resonance where the energy is, and accept the trade-off that a narrow, sharply tuned gap protects a small band very well while a broader, graded arrangement protects more bandwidth less deeply.

This work also feeds the reservoir computing thread. A lattice of bistable, viscoelastic cells is simultaneously a metamaterial and a physical reservoir — the same hardware, asked a different question.
